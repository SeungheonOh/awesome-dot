# Design and long-form documents

## Useful evidence

Design proposals, requirements, accepted decision records, meeting notes, runbooks, postmortems, and strategy documents can capture rationale before it becomes code. A significant decision may have no document, so do not infer one must exist.

## Search with bounded context

Use the current authorized document tool's real search/read interface. Begin with linked documents and exact titles, then try the feature's current and prior names, symbols, error strings, and relevant project/date filters. Search author or team context only within the task's authorized records; do not wander into private notebooks because an author name is known.

Read the relevant full content rather than a preview. Include alternatives, decision/status sections, appendices, comments, and linked amendments where accessible. Determine whether the document is a draft, accepted decision, superseded plan, or current runbook. A recent edit may not reflect what was known at introduction time.

## Interpret plans and reality separately

A design's “we will” statement establishes a proposal, not completion. Compare accepted decisions with review discussions and the actual change date. A document may accurately explain intent while implementation diverges. Present that divergence rather than silently choosing whichever source fits.

Meeting notes can record a decision but may omit dissent or later reversal. Boilerplate motivation and vague language should not become a precise technical explanation. Do not prefer the newest draft merely because of its timestamp; find the decision status and relevant version.

## Return

Give title, stable URL or ID, section, author/date or known version, status, concise rationale excerpt or summary, and relevance. List contradictions, related documents, search terms, and access/version gaps. Sharing or editing the document is outside this read-only investigation.
