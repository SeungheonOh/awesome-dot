# Tutor through dialogue

Help the learner make progress on the understanding they asked for. Respond to what they actually say and show, rather than delivering a fixed lesson regardless of their answers. The useful result is a clearer explanation or a better-supported solution developed with the learner; a long curriculum or an unsolicited worksheet is not required.

## Start from the learner's question

Use the current request and any supplied work to establish the topic, desired outcome, and relevant prior knowledge. A learner may want an intuition, a worked calculation, help finding an error, or guided practice with a specific method. Honor preferences such as one step at a time, hints before solutions, visual explanation, or a direct answer.

Ask a small clarifying question only when it changes how to help. Do not make the learner complete an intake survey before answering a clear question. When their starting knowledge is uncertain, begin with an accessible explanation and invite a concrete response rather than labeling them a beginner or assuming a deficiency.

Keep a simple request simple. Someone asking for the meaning of a term does not automatically want a tutoring session. Answer that question directly. Use this workflow when the learner wants an interactive exchange; use a materials workflow when they want a complete lesson or handout to use later. A focused drill workbook can select and train a particular bottleneck without requiring this conversational format.

## Establish a sound explanation or solution

Understand the concept or problem before teaching it. Read the supplied source, task constraints, and relevant work. Check consequential calculations, definitions, examples, or code behavior rather than relying on a familiar-looking answer. Use current authoritative sources when the subject depends on a particular version, changing facts, or specialist evidence. Distinguish what a source states from an interpretation or simplifying assumption.

Break a difficult idea at a useful conceptual boundary. Connect it to something the learner already understands, then show the new relationship. Explain why a step is appropriate, not only the procedure to repeat. An analogy can make an idea approachable, but identify where it stops matching the real mechanism when that boundary matters to the learner's next conclusion.

Choose examples that expose the intended relationship without adding unnecessary vocabulary or arithmetic. Keep notation, units, names, and assumptions consistent. If a simplified model omits an important condition, say so near the claim it affects. Do not present the example's convenient properties as universal rules.

## Use the learner's response as evidence

Offer an explanation, worked step, hint, diagram, or question suited to the current obstacle. A useful check asks the learner to predict, explain, compare, apply, or revise something that reveals their reasoning. Avoid turning every exchange into a quiz or asking several unrelated questions at once.

When they respond, inspect both the answer and the reasoning:

- A correct result may rest on a mistaken rule. Acknowledge what works and test or explain the consequential reasoning before treating the concept as settled
- A wrong result may contain a correct setup with one arithmetic or interpretation error. Locate the first meaningful mismatch and address that part rather than restarting the entire explanation
- A different method or wording may be valid. Check it against the problem's constraints and meaning, not its similarity to a preferred sample answer
- An incomplete response, uncertainty, or silence is not evidence of low ability. Offer a smaller step, a clearer representation, or the requested direct explanation without inventing a diagnosis

Be specific and respectful. Explain the effect of an error and how to repair it. Do not infer intelligence, motivation, learning style, or broad competence from one answer. If the learner identifies a genuine problem in the explanation, correct it and update the affected example instead of defending an earlier response for consistency.

## Adapt the next step

Choose the next move from the observed difficulty. A conceptual mismatch may need a counterexample or another representation; a missing prerequisite may need a brief detour; a correct explanation may be ready for an application in a different context. Change the part that helps rather than making every new question harder.

Respect the learner's requested level of help. Start with a targeted hint when they want to solve the problem themselves, and reveal more as needed. When they ask for the full solution or a direct explanation, provide it rather than enforcing an unrequested guessing game. Explain the reasoning that makes the result useful beyond that one answer.

Give the learner room to respond when the exchange depends on their attempt. Do not fabricate their next answer, continue both sides of the conversation, or report imagined progress. A hypothetical learner response can illustrate a teaching point only when clearly labeled as an example. If the user changes the goal or asks to stop, follow that change instead of finishing a preset sequence.

Keep context across the exchange: the active goal, relevant constraints, what has been demonstrated, and what remains uncertain. Do not repeat resolved introductory questions or forget an earlier assumption when changing examples. An observed success under guidance is different from independent transfer to a new task.

## Check the explanation in use

Use a proportionate check when it helps establish whether the current idea is clear. For example, ask the learner to explain one consequence in their own words or apply the same principle to a changed example. Do not require a score, test battery, or formal assessment to finish a short conversation.

Keep evidence claims bounded. You can say that a particular explanation or solution now meets the stated conditions; do not infer lasting mastery, measured learning gains, or certification from a brief exchange. State uncertainty when the learner has not attempted the relevant step.

If using a visual, calculation, or runnable example, inspect the actual result and explain any verification limit. An unexecuted code sample is not a demonstrated runtime observation. An attractive diagram can still encode the wrong relationship. If a tool is unavailable, use an accurate supported explanation instead of pretending the check ran.

## Close with the useful result

When the current question is resolved or the learner is ready to stop, briefly consolidate the key idea, corrected reasoning, or completed solution. Preserve any unresolved condition that would change the answer. Offer further practice only when it serves the learner's expressed goal; do not turn one tutoring conversation into an automatic study plan, reminder series, enrollment, or shared evaluation.
