# Plan Project Delivery

Produce a plan someone can use to begin work, coordinate handoffs, and recognize delivery. Connect the desired result to the work and evidence needed to achieve it. Scale the plan to the project: a clear small task may need a few sequenced steps and a completion check; a consequential project with unresolved dependencies needs explicit assumptions and decision points.

## Establish the planning basis

Use the request, accepted scope, existing artifacts, and relevant decisions to establish the outcome, required deliverables, acceptance criteria, and constraints. Reuse a current plan where one exists, preserving accepted commitments unless the user asks to reconsider them. Distinguish verified completed work from reported or assumed progress before planning what remains.

Do not require a formal brief when the scope is already clear. If different interpretations would materially change delivery, resolve the outcome or affected boundary before committing to that part of the plan. Continue planning independent work. Separate confirmed constraints, working assumptions, and decisions still needed; a missing date alone does not prevent a useful sequence.

Identify the planning horizon the user actually needs. Detail the work that can be specified responsibly now; leave later work at the level supported by present knowledge, with a clear condition for refining it. Do not invent a roadmap or add adjacent projects to make the plan look comprehensive.

## Plan around usable results

Choose increments that deliver something useful or test a consequential assumption. A list of components such as “content, design, development, testing” hides whether anything can actually be used. Prefer an increment such as “a reader can complete the main procedure using a draft with verified examples,” then identify the work required to produce it.

For each important deliverable or increment, make clear:

- What will exist or work, and how it advances the requested outcome
- What evidence or observable check establishes acceptance
- What must happen to produce and verify it, including a real handoff where needed

Include integration, review, revision, delivery, or transition work when they are necessary for the result to be usable. Do not defer all validation to the end or count a prepared artifact as accepted without the required check. An early partial result must retain essential safety, compatibility, and quality requirements; omitted required scope remains visible as later work.

Break work down until its next action, prerequisite, and completion condition are understandable. Avoid arbitrary task-size limits or equal-sized work packages. A small project should not acquire elaborate phases merely to fill a template.

## Connect dependencies and responsibilities

Sequence work by what genuinely enables it. State the dependency and the condition that clears it: “use the approved data definitions” is more useful than “after analysis.” Distinguish necessary prerequisites from a preferred order. Include decisions, access, external inputs, reviews, and handoffs when they affect delivery.

Show parallel work only when its prerequisites allow it. Apparent independence does not establish available capacity: shared specialists, reviewers, equipment, or environments may still constrain the sequence. Do not assume unlimited people or availability. Surface missing dependencies or circular requirements rather than silently dropping an edge; identify the clarification or changed approach that could resolve them.

Use named responsibilities only when supported by the user's instructions or existing agreements. Separate a confirmed owner from a proposed person or role; mark work unassigned when responsibility is unknown. Include a reviewer or decision maker only when their participation affects acceptance or readiness. A name in a plan does not establish that person's agreement or capacity.

## Reduce the risks that could change the plan

Prioritize early work that resolves material uncertainty, unblocks others, or produces an inspectable usable increment. Explain the reason for the order. Do not automatically begin with the easiest tasks or finish isolated components before checking that they work together.

For a significant uncertainty, identify the evidence that would resolve it, the smallest useful investigation or trial, and the decision its result enables. Give discovery work an exit condition rather than an arbitrary timebox. If the result could invalidate later work, show that later work as conditional and avoid false detail.

Keep risks specific to this project. Connect each material risk to the deliverable or milestone it threatens and an appropriate response: prevention, an early check, a fallback, or a decision to accept it. Distinguish a possible risk from a present blocker. Include recovery or reversibility where failure would be costly; do not add a generic risk register to a simple task.

## Use milestones and dates honestly

Define milestones as observable achievements or decisions, such as an accepted pilot or a verified handoff. “Phase two” or “80% complete” does not establish readiness. Each milestone should have a completion condition and its important prerequisites.

Distinguish a supplied deadline, an agreed commitment, a requested target, and a planning estimate. Use estimates only when they help the decision or the user requests them. State their basis and relevant assumptions; transparently provisional ranges are preferable to unsupported precision. Keep effort, elapsed duration, and waiting time distinct. Without enough evidence for timing, give a dependency sequence and explain what would support a date estimate.

Check proposed timing against known dependencies, availability, review delays, and constraints. Do not reverse-engineer invented durations to fit a desired date. When a deadline conflicts with the available evidence, show the tradeoff or unresolved feasibility and the decision needed. Propose a scope or sequencing change without treating it as accepted, and preserve mandatory acceptance checks.

## Deliver an actionable plan

Choose a format the user can maintain. A concise sequence works for small tasks; a table or grouped milestones may help when several people and dependencies interact. Include only fields that carry useful information, while making deliverables, checks, dependencies, responsibility gaps, and concrete next steps easy to find.

Lead with the recommended approach and the constraint that most affects delivery. Make the first ready actions explicit. For blocked work, name the missing input or decision, who can resolve it if known, and what can proceed meanwhile. Link material source decisions where useful; a separate traceability system is unnecessary unless the project needs one.

Before delivering, walk the plan from start to acceptance. Check that every required outcome has a delivery path and a check, that prerequisites have a way to be satisfied, and that no claimed date or assignment relies on an unstated assumption. Look for missing integration or handoff work and unsupported simultaneous commitments. Remove tasks that do not contribute to an outcome, a necessary dependency, or useful risk reduction.

If the user asked only for a plan, finish with the usable plan and consequential open decisions. If they also requested implementation, proceed with authorized ready work; do not create an approval gate merely because planning is complete. Creating external tasks, sending invitations, assigning work, or changing commitments requires scope that actually covers those actions and destinations. Keep proposed changes reviewable when authority is missing.

As execution supplies new evidence, revise affected steps, dependencies, and forecasts. Explain material changes and preserve the distinction between proposed, agreed, completed, and verified work. A completed plan is not evidence that the project has been delivered.
