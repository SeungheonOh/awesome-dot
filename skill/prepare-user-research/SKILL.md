# Prepare User Research

Turn a research question into materials someone can use with participants. Deliver the requested discussion guide, task cards, observation sheet or research plan, with enough context to run and interpret the work. A request for a few interview questions does not need a formal protocol or a full research program.

## Connect the question to a decision

Read the product context, candidate materials, existing evidence and constraints. Establish what the team needs to learn, why it matters, and what could change as a result. Separate the team's assumptions from established observations. “People find this confusing” is a hypothesis unless the supplied evidence supports it.

Identify the relevant people and situations: what they are trying to do, their experience with this task or product, and the contexts the research needs to cover. Preserve the user's chosen audience, method, participant availability and session constraints. Do not replace a supplied sample with an ideal recruitment plan or infer people's experience from demographics.

Use available facts before asking for more. Ask only when missing information changes the materials or interpretation, such as whether participants can use a working service or only see screenshots. Label ordinary planning assumptions and continue with independent preparation. [GOV.UK's planning guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/plan-user-research-for-your-service) connects research questions, user groups and methods; its organizational schedules and sample suggestions are not universal requirements for this task.

## Choose evidence the method can provide

Match the method to the learning goal without substituting a different assignment:

- **Interviews:** explore actual recent experiences, decisions, workarounds and context. Ask for a concrete example before accepting a broad opinion. A person's prediction about future use is different from observed adoption
- **Moderated tasks:** observe what someone tries, understands or accomplishes with the supplied product or prototype. Give a meaningful goal without naming the control, command or technique they are meant to discover
- **Combined sessions:** keep the purpose of each part clear. Early questions, explanations or demonstrations can teach answers to later tasks; sequence them with that effect in mind

State the fidelity of the materials. A written command or explanation can show understanding but cannot establish successful execution, saved state or import behavior. A static screen cannot test a feature that is absent. Keep reported experience, observed behavior, preference and interpretation separate.

If the requested method cannot answer a consequential question, explain the specific limit and propose the smallest useful adjustment. A small qualitative round can reveal problems and suggest improvements; it does not establish population rates or a causal effect merely because a few counts are available. Do not turn an ordinary research-preparation request into a statistical experiment without the necessary design and scope.

## Write materials people can actually use

Create the requested materials directly, rather than returning advice to create them later. Keep research objectives and expected observations in the moderator's material; participants should see neutral questions or tasks, not the answer key.

For an interview, organize a short sequence around the questions that matter. Start with concrete past situations and follow what the participant says with neutral probes. Avoid questions that contain the team's explanation, combine several judgments, or solicit agreement with a proposed solution. Include optional follow-ups rather than a script that requires every question regardless of the conversation. [The interview guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-in-depth-interviews) gives practical detail on using examples and flexible discussion guides.

For a task, provide the scenario, starting materials and goal the participant needs. Check that the expected outcome follows from a real contract or the study's stated criteria. Do not hide a requirement in the scoring key that the task never establishes. Leave room for another valid approach. Specify what the moderator can clarify and what would become assistance or teaching. [The moderated-testing guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/using-moderated-usability-testing) describes goal-based tasks and consistent, neutral facilitation.

Use an introduction appropriate to the actual session: purpose, what will happen, proposed note-taking or recording, and how someone can pause or stop. Use the organization's applicable participant information when supplied. Do not invent retention promises, consent status or a legal assurance. Planning materials do not establish that participants have agreed or that contact, recording, collection or sharing is authorized.

Make the live note sheet easy to use while listening. Capture the participant/task context, what happened or was said, relevant assistance and unresolved points. Put detailed answer keys, coding legends and later interpretation outside the live sheet when they would overload it. Label coding and synthesis as work after the session, unless the design genuinely requires a live measurement. Quotes require an exact record; otherwise label notes as paraphrases.

## Keep comparisons interpretable

When comparing versions, identify the exact candidates and the intended comparison. Different content, capabilities or instructions can explain a difference; do not call it a wording-only effect when those also changed. Surface known omissions separately without silently editing the candidates or declaring how participants will respond.

If the same people see multiple versions, account for order and learning. Use an appropriate order allocation and comparable task details where feasible, while acknowledging residual carryover and any imbalance. Do not teach the first task's answers before the second and then treat the second as independent first use. Keep first answers distinct from answers after probes or hints.

Define observations that bear on the decision before collecting them. Preserve unsuccessful, unfinished, skipped and unavailable observations separately. Repeated exposures from the same people are not additional independent participants. Preferences can inform a decision, but do not erase consequential misunderstandings. Avoid an arbitrary winning score that conceals tradeoffs or missing evidence.

## Check and hand over a workable packet

Read through the materials as a moderator and as a participant. Check that tasks can be attempted with the actual materials, prompts do not disclose the answer, examples are internally consistent, and instructions fit the requested session length. Prioritize the essential questions; leave optional probes clearly optional. Timing arithmetic is a planning check, not proof that participants will finish on time.

For a more complex session, a permitted practice run can reveal awkward wording or setup problems. Report exactly what was tried. An internal read-through, an AI-generated response or a fictional example is not participant research and must not become a quotation, finding or measured success rate.

Deliver the ready materials with the few unresolved preparation choices that matter. State how observations will be compared or synthesized, what would justify a revision, and when the result should remain inconclusive. Keep contrary evidence and the limits of the sample, method and prototype visible. Do not force a winner or add participants merely to reach a preferred conclusion.

Stop at the preparation the user requested. Recruiting, contacting or scheduling people, collecting their data, enabling recording, publishing a form and distributing findings are separate actions that need their own actual scope and authority. Preserve the original product materials and distinguish a prepared study from one that has run.
