# Create learning materials

Make the materials a learner or facilitator can actually use. A topic list or suggested lesson structure is not a completed lesson when the user asked for the teaching content. If the request is only for a plan, activity, or revision to one section, keep that scope instead of building a whole course.

## Define the useful learning outcome

Establish who the material is for, what they should be able to explain or do afterward, and what prior knowledge they can reasonably use. Draw this from the request and supplied context; ask only when a missing answer materially changes the content or difficulty. Do not require a diagnostic test before a clear request, or infer a personal weakness from missing work samples.

Respect the intended setting: live facilitation, independent study, a short explanation with practice, or another requested format. Note consequential constraints such as available tools, reading level, time, group interaction, and whether learners should see answers immediately. Treat an estimated session length as a planning constraint, not evidence that the material has been timed with participants.

Keep the outcome observable enough to shape the material. “Understand percentages” is less useful than being able to distinguish a percentage-point change from a relative percentage change in a short report. Do not invent a fixed number of objectives or expand a small task into a curriculum. When the goal spans too much for the requested scope, propose the most useful bounded treatment and make what it leaves out clear.

## Build an accurate, connected explanation

Use supplied authoritative material and current primary sources when the subject requires them. Separate settled facts, conventions, simplifications, and genuine disagreement. Define terms consistently, and retain qualifications that affect the learner's conclusions. Do not present an analogy as a literal mechanism or silently substitute a simplified rule for the real one.

Arrange the concepts so that each step uses ideas already introduced or explicitly assumed. Start from a situation or question the learner can recognize, then explain the principle and show it operating. Choose examples whose details illuminate the target idea rather than distracting with unnecessary domain knowledge.

Work through consequential steps, including why a choice is made and how to check its result. Merely giving a polished final answer can hide the exact reasoning the lesson is meant to teach. Conversely, do not bury a simple idea in every exception at once. Introduce an exception when it changes a likely use, and distinguish an introductory simplification from a general guarantee.

Adapt the teaching medium to the content. A diagram can clarify a relationship, a small executable example can expose behavior, and a short comparison can distinguish easily confused concepts. Use those forms when they help; do not add slides, games, quizzes, or worksheets to every request. When a native file or working example is requested, create that artifact with suitable available tools.

## Give learners something meaningful to do

Choose activities that require the intended understanding rather than repeating a phrase or recognizing the formatting of the example. The activity may be a prediction, explanation, decision, construction, critique, calculation, or practical task. Include the inputs, instructions, and resources needed to attempt it without guessing an unstated rule.

Match support to the learner and setting. A first task may retain a worked structure or offer a hint; a later task can change the context so the learner must choose how to apply the idea. Do not force every lesson into a baseline-and-drill sequence. If the user wants a workbook built around selecting and practicing a bottleneck through focused drills, keep that practice workflow primary; the bottleneck need not already be known.

Provide feedback the learner can act on. For a determinate task, include the answer and the reasoning or checks that establish it. For an open task, give the qualities that make a response effective, relevant alternatives, and a worked example without treating its wording as the only valid answer. Explain a likely wrong turn where it clarifies the concept, but do not attribute that mistake to the learner without an actual attempt.

Respect the requested answer-reveal behavior. Separate hints and solutions from the attempt when they should not be seen first, and make both easy to find afterward. A facilitator's notes can contain prompts and discussion points that do not belong on the learner sheet. Keep this separation useful rather than creating a large packet of documents by default.

## Make the material usable in its setting

For a live session, give the facilitator enough guidance to run the actual activities: what to show or ask, what learners produce, useful discussion points, and how to move on. Include time estimates where the request calls for them, with room for questions or transitions. Do not promise that a detailed agenda fits a duration merely because its estimates add up.

For independent use, make prerequisites, instructions, examples, practice, and feedback available in a navigable order. Avoid directions that require an absent facilitator or a tool the learner is not expected to have. Offer a supported alternative when it preserves the outcome, and identify a genuine prerequisite when it cannot.

Use vocabulary, notation, and layout consistently. Check that a task does not accidentally reveal its answer in the neighboring example or depend on content omitted from the learner version. Keep optional extension material distinguishable from what is needed to achieve the stated goal.

## Check the teaching content and deliver it

Solve the tasks using only the information and permitted resources the learner receives. Check worked examples and answers independently enough to catch arithmetic, logical, factual, or execution errors. When a task admits several valid approaches, verify that the feedback accepts them. Repair contradictory or impossible instructions rather than blaming an imagined learner.

Review whether the activities actually exercise the promised outcome and whether the explanation gives a fair route to it. A vocabulary quiz alone cannot establish that someone can perform a practical task. No completed attempt means no measured learner performance; author checks and hypothetical responses do not demonstrate learning gains, mastery, or certification.

Reopen saved materials and inspect the presentation the learner will receive, including readable diagrams, complete tables, usable links, answer boundaries, and any requested native editability. Execute supplied code or calculations when available and within scope, and identify unrun checks precisely. If the format or tool cannot be produced, provide the supported material without labeling it as the missing artifact.

Deliver the requested learner materials and only the facilitator or answer support needed to use them. Briefly state important assumptions, what was checked, and any remaining prerequisite or source uncertainty. Creating a lesson does not itself conduct a session, assess real participants, enroll anyone, or arrange a recurring course.
