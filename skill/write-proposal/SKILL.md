# Write a Proposal

Produce the proposal the user needs to put a concrete choice in front of its intended reader. Explain the problem, the recommended action, why it is justified, what it requires, and what decision is being requested. Persuasive writing should make the reasoning easy to assess, not make uncertain benefits sound guaranteed.

## Establish the decision and brief

Read the request, supplied evidence, relevant prior decisions, and any required template. Identify the audience, the decision they are being asked to make, and what the user has already chosen. Distinguish developing a recommendation from expressing one the user supplied.

Use the requested length, tone, format, and destination. Infer ordinary editorial choices from context. Ask only when a missing fact would change the recommendation, permitted scope, or a material commitment. A missing cost can remain an explicit gap in a draft; do not invent it to complete a template.

If a proposal responds to a stated brief or request for proposals, account for its actual questions, evaluation criteria, required sections, and submission constraints. Do not add promises or terms merely because a standard proposal template contains them.

## Build the case from evidence

Describe the current problem using concrete observations and its consequence for the intended audience. Separate a demonstrated problem from a hypothesis about its cause. Do not create urgency, demand, endorsements, or a return on investment that the sources do not support.

Connect the recommended action to that problem. Explain the mechanism: what changes, for whom, and why that is expected to help. Preserve constraints the user has already selected. Consider a credible alternative, including the current approach, when it helps the reader understand the tradeoff; an exhaustive options analysis is not required for every proposal.

Keep facts, assumptions, forecasts, and recommendations distinguishable. Cite material evidence close to the claim it supports. Recalculate meaningful totals or comparisons from their actual inputs, and keep units, periods, exclusions, and one-time versus recurring costs clear. If a benefit is only expected, state the basis and what would establish it.

Address the strongest relevant objection or limitation fairly. Do not hide a dependency, implementation cost, unsupported assumption, or less favorable result in an appendix while the main text implies certainty. A narrower pilot or conditional recommendation may be more credible than an unsupported full rollout, but do not substitute it for the user's explicit choice without explaining why.

## Make the action concrete

Describe the proposed scope, useful deliverables, important dependencies, and how success would be judged. Include enough of the delivery approach for the reader to understand feasibility. Use a separate detailed project plan only when the request needs one.

State requested resources and known responsibilities without inventing another person's availability or agreement. Distinguish confirmed dates and commitments from proposed targets. Identify exclusions that prevent a likely misunderstanding, rather than padding the document with a generic list of things not included.

Write an explicit decision request: what the reader is asked to approve, choose, fund, review, or authorize, and what would happen next if they agree. Keep any conditions attached. Do not turn a draft request into a claim that approval, funding, capacity, or delivery has already been secured.

For customer-facing or otherwise consequential proposals, preserve supplied pricing, scope, validity conditions, and terms exactly where required. Flag missing or conflicting terms instead of creating legal or commercial commitments in the user's voice.

## Draft for the reader

Lead with the recommendation and the reason it matters. Develop the evidence, proposed action, tradeoffs, resources, and decision request in an order the audience can follow. Use headings, tables, or an appendix only where they help the reader compare or act. Keep the main proposal understandable without opening the supporting material.

Write the complete text, not only an outline or a list of advice. Match the user's voice when evidence of it is available, without inventing personal opinions or experiences. Prefer concrete language to promotional claims such as “transformative,” “seamless,” or “best in class.”

Maintain a clear distinction between the proposal and private drafting questions. Use comments or a short handoff note for unresolved facts when appropriate; do not put an unsupported placeholder in polished prose where a reader could mistake it for an agreed fact.

## Check and deliver

Read the proposal as a skeptical member of the intended audience. Can they identify the requested decision, understand the evidence, see the material costs and conditions, and tell what happens next? Check that the recommendation addresses the original problem and every required part of the brief is answered or explicitly unresolved.

Verify decisive numbers, citations, names, and commitments against the sources. Check that shortening the document has not removed the qualification that makes a claim accurate. If editing a native template or creating a file, inspect the saved artifact for intact structure, readable layout, and functioning references as supported by the available tools.

Deliver the actual proposal in the requested format and explain only the material choices or gaps that need the user's attention. Drafting does not itself authorize submission, external sharing, signature, or a commitment. Complete an explicitly authorized delivery within its actual audience and scope, and verify the result before saying it was sent or published.
