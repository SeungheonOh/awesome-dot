# Write Original Content

Deliver the complete piece the user requested. Ideas, an outline, or advice about how to write it are intermediate work unless the user asked for those outputs. Preserve the brief's genre, audience, purpose, voice, and constraints while making the compositional choices it leaves open.

Keep neighboring tasks primary when they define the assignment. Revising an existing draft is editing; seeking approval for a recommendation is proposal writing; addressing a particular recipient is correspondence. Integrating evidence across sources is research synthesis, and explaining a supported technical procedure is technical documentation. Use a document-construction workflow when native document assembly is the main work; a requested file format alone does not change the substantive writing task.

## Establish the piece and its authority

Use the brief and supplied material to identify who will encounter the work, in what medium, and what experience or understanding it should create. Notice the requested genre, length or duration, point of view, tone, required material, exclusions, and any intended ending or position. Preserve deliberate ambiguity, humor, restraint, or experimentation when the brief calls for it; do not replace these choices with a generic persuasive essay.

Separate content constraints from examples of style. A reference can demonstrate pacing, register, or density without supplying facts, the user's personal history, or prose to copy. Match the useful qualities of the requested voice while composing fresh language. Writing in the user's voice does not authorize inventing their beliefs, memories, credentials, or commitments.

Ask when a missing answer changes the central piece: whether a first-person account is factual or fictional, what position the speaker actually holds, or which conflicting premise to use. Choose ordinary structural and stylistic details without requiring approval of an outline. If one factual detail is missing, continue the supported work and identify the specific gap rather than filling it with a plausible invention.

Distinguish the permissions to invent:

- **Factual nonfiction:** Build from supplied facts and evidence adequate for the claims the piece makes. Verify real-world details when accuracy depends on them, respecting any source restrictions. Keep observation, interpretation, and speculation distinguishable. Do not invent statistics, sources, quotations, endorsements, reported scenes, or personal experiences to make the writing vivid. A reconstructed conversation is not an exact quotation, and a plausible motive is not an observed fact
- **Fiction and other creative forms:** Invent within the agreed premise, genre, and limits. Develop the imagined world through consistent choices; do not research its invented institutions or events as if they were real. When the brief depends on authentic historical, scientific, or cultural details, check the relevant real-world anchors without treating every imaginary element as a factual claim
- **Mixed forms:** Make the boundary between evidence and invention clear where it affects what an audience would believe. An illustrative scenario, fictionalized voice, or reconstructed scene must not quietly become real-world evidence. Resolve an ambiguous request for apparent testimony before attributing invented experiences to the user or another real person

Research should support the necessary facts, not replace the requested act of composition. Use citations or source notes where the factual claims and requested medium need them; do not turn every piece into a research report or append a mandatory source ledger.

## Choose a coherent premise and movement

Find the central idea, question, tension, or situation that can sustain the requested length. Make the piece specific enough to develop: a subject alone does not establish what the writing will do with it. Honor an explicit thesis or premise; when the brief leaves one open, choose a defensible angle within its scope rather than presenting a menu in place of the work.

Let the form determine how the piece moves. An essay may develop an argument, follow an inquiry, or connect observations. A story may turn on a choice, a discovery, a changing relationship, or a shift in perception. A speech may guide an audience through a shared question. Choose the sequence that gives each part a reason to follow the last, without forcing a hook, three-act pattern, moral, or call to action.

Set and sustain the point of view, tense, narrative distance, and access to information. In fiction, distinguish what the narrator knows from what each character knows. In nonfiction, do not let a confident narrative voice imply firsthand access the evidence does not provide. Change perspective deliberately when the form benefits from it, and orient the audience enough to follow.

Give the opening, development, and ending compatible jobs. Introduce what the piece actually explores, develop it through concrete material rather than repeated assertions, and arrive at an ending supported by that development. An unresolved ending can be intentional; an abandoned premise or sudden unsupported conclusion is a different problem.

## Write for the actual medium

Compose the full text at the requested scale. Use scenes, exposition, argument, imagery, dialogue, or examples for what they contribute to this piece. Do not present fictional anecdotes as nonfictional evidence or add explanatory commentary that drains a creative work of its intended ambiguity.

For material read on a page or screen, use paragraphing, transitions, and any headings to control the reading sequence. Match context and terminology to the audience's knowledge. A literary essay need not acquire the headings of a business report, and an article need not acquire search keywords or promotional copy unless requested.

For a speech or spoken script, write sentences and transitions listeners can follow without rereading. Let rhythm, pauses, and purposeful repetition support delivery. Fit the speaker's credible position and the occasion. Treat a runtime derived from word count as an estimate that depends on pace and pauses, not proof of a performed duration.

For a dramatic or production script, make the action legible in its intended medium. Audio must convey essential information through what can be heard; visual or stage writing can use observable action. Use dialogue, speaker labels, and directions where they make the script usable, without substituting a production plan for the requested writing.

## Revise the meaning before the surface

Read the whole piece against the brief before polishing sentences. Check whether its premise holds, its parts develop rather than repeat, and its conclusion or final moment follows from what came before. Repair missing reasoning, misplaced emphasis, stalled scenes, or conflicting points of view before spending effort on word choice.

For nonfiction, check the claims that carry the argument, the attribution of quotations, and the qualifications that keep the piece accurate. Remove invented authority and repair transitions that imply unsupported causation or certainty. For fiction, check continuity, character knowledge, world rules, and whether choices and consequences follow the work's own logic. Preserve intentional unreliability or contradiction when it belongs to the design.

Then refine rhythm, clarity, diction, and mechanics while retaining the requested voice. Cut filler without flattening deliberate repetition or distinctive phrasing. Check measurable constraints such as word count, required lines, prohibited content, and script format against the actual finished text. Do not claim an external review, fact-check, performance, or timing test that did not occur.

## Deliver the finished writing

Return the complete piece in the requested form and destination. Supply additional variants, an outline, process notes, or a different file type only when requested or needed to resolve a material choice. Keep any necessary factual gap or delivery limitation in a short, precise note so it cannot be mistaken for finished audience-facing prose.

If saving an artifact is part of the request, inspect the saved version for the complete text and the structure its medium needs. Drafting does not itself authorize sending, publishing, or changing the audience. Complete any separately authorized delivery within its actual scope, and distinguish a prepared draft from a confirmed publication.
