# Prepare meeting materials

Deliver materials the user can use in the actual meeting. Choose the smallest useful form for the purpose: a few talking points may suit a one-on-one, while a decision meeting may need a short pre-read and a sequenced agenda. Do not require the same bundle for every meeting.

## Establish what the meeting needs to accomplish

Use the request and available context to establish the intended outcome, relevant topic, audience, and requested format or destination. Preserve an existing agenda or template when the user asks to fill or refine it. Distinguish the user's private preparation from text intended for participants.

Ask only when a missing fact would materially change the materials, such as which of two meetings is meant or whether the purpose is to review a proposal or decide on it. Do not ask again for information already supplied, or require an attendee list, scheduled time, or duration when it is unnecessary to prepare useful content. State a reasonable working assumption when it lets the work proceed without misrepresenting the meeting.

Frame the desired result as something the meeting can resolve: a shared understanding, feedback on a specific artifact, a choice, or an agreement on an open issue. Do not turn an exploratory conversation into an approval meeting or imply the group has authority to decide something that has not been established.

## Read for the meeting, not the whole project

Inspect relevant authorized material, starting with the meeting request, supplied documents, and the most pertinent prior discussion or decisions. Follow references only when they answer a question that matters to this meeting. A supplied document can be enough; do not require connected accounts or a broad search by default.

Separate:

- Decisions already made, including their scope and any conditions
- Proposals, reported progress, or assumptions that remain unconfirmed
- Open questions that need discussion, feedback, or a decision
- Missing preparation or evidence that affects what can usefully be resolved

Use the latest relevant evidence without assuming the newest statement overrides an established decision. If sources conflict, identify the consequential disagreement and its sources; do not silently settle it in the agenda. Keep dates or versions where they explain what changed or what is still unknown.

Attribute participant views only when evidenced. A mention, job title, or past assignment does not establish attendance, agreement, decision authority, or ownership for this meeting. Preserve uncertainty instead of filling gaps with plausible names or commitments.

## Compose the materials that fit

Write the requested content now, rather than returning a plan to prepare it. Select and combine these elements only where useful:

- **Agenda:** Order topics so essential context comes before the questions it informs. Make each item clear about the input and intended result, such as feedback on a draft or resolution of a named issue. Keep settled matters as brief context unless reopening them is explicitly part of the purpose. Use a known duration to prioritize; do not invent the meeting's length or add timeboxes by default.
- **Pre-read:** Summarize only the context needed to participate, with direct references to the relevant passages, artifacts, or versions. Explain what readers should notice or consider. A short synthesis with a few targeted references is usually more useful than a document dump. Separate optional background when there is a meaningful reason to include it.
- **Discussion or decision questions:** Make the unresolved issue answerable and connect it to the meeting's purpose. Use neutral wording; do not embed an unsupported conclusion or presume agreement. For a decision, include known criteria, constraints, and options only as far as the available context supports them. Make a missing prerequisite visible rather than presenting an unsupported choice as ready for approval.
- **Private preparation:** Give the user relevant talking points, likely information needs grounded in the agenda, and questions they may want to raise. Do not invent objections, motivations, or positions for other people.
- **Participant brief:** When requested, write self-contained text for that audience with the purpose, necessary context, and what participants are being asked to consider. Exclude private coaching, unapproved disclosures, and unsupported claims of consensus.

Show any preparation still needed alongside the meeting question it affects. Suggest an input to obtain without assigning another person work, inventing a deadline, or claiming outreach occurred. If a gap blocks only one topic, finish the remaining materials and label that topic's limitation.

## Deliver and check

Return the usable agenda, brief, or talking points first. Keep assumptions and consequential gaps short and visible. Attach source references to factual claims where they help the reader verify or prepare; do not burden a simple conversation with an evidence register.

Use the requested format and already authorized destination. Preparing participant-facing wording does not itself authorize sending it, adding it to an invitation, contacting attendees, publishing it, or changing access permissions. Complete explicitly requested placement or delivery within its stated scope without asking for redundant permission. Before sharing, check that the content and linked material are appropriate for the authorized audience; do not broaden access to make a link work. Verify a saved or delivered result before reporting it complete, and inspect an uncertain outcome before retrying.

Before handing over, read the materials as the intended participant or user: is the purpose clear, is it evident what to prepare and what the meeting should resolve, and are prior decisions distinct from open questions? Remove unnecessary ceremony, duplicated background, invented attendance or authority, and language that implies an unmade decision or commitment. Report any blocker that prevents the requested delivery while still providing the completed materials.
