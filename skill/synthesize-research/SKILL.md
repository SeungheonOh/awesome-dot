# Synthesize Research

Produce a coherent answer to the user's question, supported by the material actually inspected. Organize the result around findings and their relationships, so the reader can understand what the evidence establishes without assembling separate source summaries.

## Set the question and evidence boundary

Use the request and available context to identify the question, intended reader, relevant population or setting, time boundary, and requested depth and format. Break a broad question into the few subquestions needed to answer it. Keep these tied to the requested outcome; interesting adjacent topics need not become new research work.

Ask only when an unresolved detail would materially change the answer, source selection, or deliverable. For example, evidence about novice users may not answer a question about experienced professionals. Ordinary assumptions can be stated and used when they are easy to revise. Do not require a research intake form or a predetermined number of sources.

Respect the source mode:

- **Supplied sources only:** Work within the packet. Identify what it can establish and which missing evidence would matter. Do not supplement it silently or describe its contents as representative of the whole field
- **Live research:** Retrieve relevant sources through authorized access. Search toward the subquestions and consequential gaps, including plausible contrary evidence. Use searches that do not disclose private context unnecessarily
- **Mixed:** Distinguish supplied material from newly retrieved evidence when their origin, dates, or selection affects the interpretation

For a historical question, distinguish evidence available by the cutoff from later evidence about that period. Do not use a current page as proof of its earlier wording without an inspectable historical version.

## Read for findings, not source prestige

Open the material supporting the answer, including methods, tables, qualifications, or linked originals when they affect a central finding. Search snippets and abstracts can locate evidence, but support only what they actually show. Record inaccessible or partially read material as a coverage limit rather than reconstructing it from a title or citation.

Choose evidence appropriate to each subquestion. An official document may establish a stated policy; observed outcomes require evidence about practice. An original study can establish what it measured under its conditions; a relevant review may reveal the broader pattern and its limitations. Consider directness, methodological fit, applicability, completeness, and incentives rather than treating source type or recency as a universal ranking.

Keep enough working notes to recover each important finding: its source and passage, relevant version or date, setting or population, measure, and material limitations. Use a small table only when it helps reconcile the evidence. A large mandatory schema adds little to a short synthesis.

Trace shared evidence. Reports that quote the same study, reuse the same dataset, or copy a press release do not provide fully independent confirmation. Reviews may overlap with each other and with included studies. Several interpretations of one result can broaden the discussion without increasing the number of independent observations. Preserve that distinction without demanding a complete citation graph.

## Reconcile the evidence

Group findings by subquestion, explanation, or meaningful pattern. Within each group:

- Compare definitions, populations, settings, methods, versions, and measurement periods before calling results consistent or contradictory
- Preserve units, denominators, baselines, and absolute versus relative changes. Do not average incompatible measurements or pool results without a justified method
- Distinguish reported observations, source interpretations, and your own synthesis. Keep association separate from causal explanation, and describe what the study design can support
- Investigate differences that could change the answer. Check corrections and relevant versions; separate publication or update dates from when events or measurements occurred
- Weigh evidence by its relevance and quality, not by counting supportive citations. Strongly unequal evidence does not require equal space or a compromise conclusion

Resolve an apparent contradiction when a supported difference explains it. If results remain inconsistent on comparable terms, state the disagreement and its consequence for the answer. Offer a possible explanation as a hypothesis when the sources do not test it. Do not invent motives or imply a disputed question has been settled.

Distinguish evidence of absence from an absence of evidence. “No effect detected” may reflect limited precision, while “not addressed in this packet” describes coverage. Neither automatically means the effect does not exist. Explain uncertainty through concrete limits such as indirect evidence, small samples, missing populations, unstable definitions, or unresolved conflict rather than invented confidence scores.

## Write the answer

Lead with the strongest supported answer and its important conditions. Then explain the findings that establish it, how they fit together, and where they diverge. Use source-by-source discussion only when a source's distinct role or method helps explain the result.

Attach citations to the claims they actually support. Prefer direct links or authorized file references with useful page, section, table, or timestamp locators. A citation at the end of a paragraph should not appear to support unrelated assertions. When the original cannot be inspected, attribute the claim to the source that reports it and make the gap visible. Label reasoning that combines several sources as synthesis rather than attributing it to any single author.

Match detail to the requested output. A short answer may need only its conclusion, a few connected findings, and consequential caveats. A longer report can separate the main explanation from source or method notes. Include a concise coverage statement identifying the evidence boundary, relevant dates, and gaps that could affect the conclusion. Do not imply an exhaustive search, systematic review, or field-wide consensus without the work needed to establish it.

End with the answer's practical meaning for the stated question and any unresolved issue that could change it. Do not force a recommendation, rank options, or prescribe further research when the user asked for understanding and the evidence already supports a useful answer.

## Check and finish

Check that every requested subquestion has an answer or a specific gap. Revisit the decisive citations, scope qualifications, and strongest contrary evidence. Confirm that repeated reporting has not become independent corroboration and that the conclusion does not extend beyond the studied settings. If it depends on one weak or inaccessible source, make that dependence clear.

Stop searching when the central findings have adequate support or explicit gaps, consequential disagreements have been examined within scope, and additional retrieval is unlikely to change the requested answer. Limited access can justify a qualified result; it does not justify claiming that no evidence exists. If a missing input prevents the central answer, identify that input while delivering the useful supported portion.

Deliver the completed answer or report in the requested format and authorized destination, rather than only a bibliography or research plan. When saving a file, inspect the saved result and its references before reporting success. Research does not itself authorize contacting sources, buying access, publication, or recurring monitoring; carry out any such step only within the user's authorization.
